I am a weirdo who loses sleep over thinking about new ways to optimize the guitar loops process. Few people have though about optimizing the guitar loop process as much as I have. These are all of my tricks that I use in Ableton to make cooking fire guitar loops a breeze.

## 1. Use a Template

The easiest way to avoid spending countless hours clowning around fiddling with settings, adjusting inputs, and setting up tracks is to use an Ableton template. This helps avoid the irritating and uninspiring parts of making music and makes it so we can get right to cooking. I mean really, who likes having to name the same tracks over and over again? Bozos do 🤡.

### Here is a breakdown of my template:

    •	**Input 1 Group**: This group of four tracks is set up to record from Input 1, which I reserve for direct-input, (DI) electric guitar. I have the tracks labeled Chords, Riff, Melody, and Countermelody. Each track has an EQ and compressor ready to go.

    •	**Input 2 Group**: This is basically the same as the Input 1 Group, but for Input 2, which I reserve for my Shure SM7B. This is where I record my acoustic and nylon string guitars.

      •	**Shortcut Buddy**: This is a Max for Live plugin that allows you to set hotkeys to open up plugins. I have it set so I can open an EQ by pressing the "8" key, reverb by pressing the "r" key, etc. This drastically cuts down on the amount of clicks you have to make.

## 2. Follow a Checklist and Automate

Here’s a little secret: Before I even touch my guitar, I’ve already mapped out my guitar loops, from the key and tempo to the loop’s name. All I have to do is follow a checklist and then improvise a melody. That's it.

In my article, <MyLink href="https://blog.yayadean.com/posts/how-javascript-changed-how-i-write-music">How Learning JavaScript Changed How I Write Guitar Loops,</MyLink> I discuss how much of the guitar loop writing process is unimportant and can be automated. Here's how I approach guitar loop writing like an assembly line:

### The Guitar Loop Checklist (dumbed down version):

    1.	**Pick a chord progression.**
    2.	**Record a chord layer.** We record chords first because it is impossible to start with a melody. There are freaks that exist that start with melody, but they are outliers.
    3.	**Write a riff:** The riff is the backbone of our loop and sets a strong foundation to write melodies and countermelodies over.
    4.	**Write a melody and countermelody:** These are what we want to spend the most focus on. They can't be automated and they are what make our loop special.
    5.	**Quantize:** Tighten everything up, but don’t lose that human feel. A little bit of imperfection is what makes it sound real.
    6.  **Mix:** Balance your levels, tweak your EQ, and sprinkle on some effects to taste.
    7.	**Arrange and Bounce:** Send an outline of all of your parts for a producer to play with on their own.

## 3. Use Ableton's Loop Recording

There are few things that irritate me more than having to press record every take. It took me two years of using Ableton to realize it has an awesome loop recording feature built right in. This means we only have to press record once, and we can play as many takes as we need to get the perfect one, or play a bunch of decent takes and comp them together.

## 4. Use Complex Warping Tastefully

Something that Ableton is known for is its ability to quantize audio at a very high quality. Ableton has several different built-in quantizing methods. The ones that tend to work the best for melodies is Complex or Complex Pro.

Warping in Ableton is kind of like adding spices to a dish—it can take something bland and turn it into something extraordinary. But, as with any seasoning, a heavy hand can ruin the whole thing.

    •	Quantizing Rhythm Parts: When it comes to rhythm guitar, I’ve learned that Complex warping can sometimes turn my crunchy riff into mush. Instead, I use Complex mode to catch those rhythmic transients, like a skilled baker catching the right moment when bread is perfectly risen. Then, I’ll tweak the timing manually, keeping the groove but tightening it up.
    •	Quantizing Melodies: Melodies, on the other hand, benefit from a bit more warping love. I’ll dial Complex mode up to around 90-95%, which is just enough to smooth things out without losing that natural, human touch. It’s like adding just the right amount of reverb—enough to enhance, not overpower.

## 5. Use One Guitar Type

When it comes to creating cohesive loops, less is more. That’s why I stick to one guitar type per loop, whether it’s nylon, acoustic, or electric. Blending guitars types, in my experience, makes it much more challenging to create a cohesive loop and also slows you down in the creative process.

By limiting yourself to one guitar, you eliminate unnecessary decisions and keep the focus on creating something truly special.

## Conclusion

And there you have it! These are all the tips and tricks I use in Ableton Live. Don't feel like you need to use all of them at once. Pick one or two that you think will help you the most and start there. Remember, the goal is to make music, not to spend hours fiddling with settings.
