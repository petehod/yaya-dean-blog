Becoming a software engineer changed the way I think about music. In software development, there are data structures called objects, which can be thought of as a collection of key and value pairs. These keys and value pairs are wrapped inside of `{}` (curly braces) and look something like this:

<CodeBlock caption="These are some details about me represented as an object. Name, age, and occupation are the keys and Dean, 25, and Software Engineer are the values.">
    ```ts
    {
        name: "Dean",
        age: 25,
        occupation: "Software Engineer"
    }
    ```
</CodeBlock>

What I realized is that _songs_ can be thought of as objects. There are a set of keys that describe a song, and each key has a value. Here is an example of a song represented as an object:

<CodeBlock caption={`"Baby" by Justin Bieber represented as an object`}>
    ```ts
    {
        song_name: "Baby",
        artist: "Justin Bieber",
        featured_artist: "Ludacris",
        tempo: 130,
        key: "Eb Major",
        chord_progression: "Eb, C-, Ab, Bb",
        time_signature: "4/4",
        melody: ?? 
        // There are many more key/value pairs that could be added. 
        // This is just a small subset.
    }
    ```
</CodeBlock>

### Automating the Process

In software development, efficiency is key. One of the most valuable lessons I’ve learned as a software engineer is the importance of automating repetitive tasks.
By automating what can be automated, we free up our mental bandwidth for more complex and creative challenges.

When I approach music production, I apply the same principle. Many aspects of a song—like tempo, key, chord progressions, and scales—can be predetermined. By setting
these parameters in advance, I create a sort of “assembly line” for producing guitar loops. This approach allows me to focus on the parts of music that truly require human creativity, like crafting melodies.

For example, before I even touch my guitar, I’ll decide on the tempo, key, and basic chord progression. In a way, this is like setting up a skeleton or framework that
I can build upon. This predetermined structure acts like an object in software development: it has defined keys and values that guide the overall process.

### The Art of Melody

While many aspects of a song can be automated or pre-planned, melody is different. Melody is the heart and soul of a song, and it’s something that can’t be easily
quantified or generated by a machine. This is where I choose to spend the majority of my creative energy.

In software development, there are certain tasks that require human intuition, creativity, and problem-solving—things that can’t be reduced to a simple algorithm.
Similarly, in music, melody requires a deep connection to the emotional core of a piece. It’s not just about hitting the right notes; it’s about creating something that resonates on a deeper level.

<Spacer size={"md"} />
### Speeding Up Production

By automating the more predictable elements of a song, I’m able to speed up the production process significantly. This “assembly line” approach allows me to pound out melodies faster than I ever could
before. I no longer have to spend hours deciding on a chord progression or figuring out the right key. Instead, I can focus on what really matters: making the melody as compelling as possible.

<Spacer size={"md"} />
### Conclusion

Learning software development has fundamentally changed the way I approach music. It’s taught me to value efficiency and to focus my creative energy on the aspects of music that can’t be automated.
By setting up an “assembly line” for guitar loop production, I’m able to streamline the process and produce music more quickly, all while ensuring that the most important part—the melody—gets the
attention it deserves.
